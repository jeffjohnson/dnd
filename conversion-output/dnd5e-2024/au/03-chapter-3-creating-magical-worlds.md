# Chapter 3: Creating Magical Worlds


![In highly magical worlds, people use magic for major tasks such as construction. (Art: Joshua Raphael)](book/AU/028-03-001.ch-3-splash.webp)

Using the advice and tools in this chapter, you can create your own world infused with magic for your D&D games. This chapter also provides guidelines for tailoring a group patron for your characters' adventures, helping the players feel immersed in your setting. When creating your magical world, you can choose the options you like best or modify an existing world using your favorite advice. For example, you could use an archmage group patron of your own creation in your adventures that take place in the Forgotten Realms.

*[Page 47]*

## Magic in Existing Worlds

If you want to use this chapter's advice within an existing world, consider the ones on the D&D Settings table. It describes the magic prevalent in each setting as well as where you can find more information about those settings.

*[Page 47]*

## Building a Magical World

The advice presented here will help you build your own magical world. Each of the sections that follow focuses on a different aspect of world-building, beginning with the broadest concepts and proceeding to specific topics, including questions designed to guide your creativity. Examples from throughout the D&D multiverse illustrate approaches to each of these subjects:

### Outline Your World

![Performers infuse their music with magic to create a joyous environment. (Art: Eilene Cherie)](book/AU/029-03-002.magic-performers.webp)

Before you create individual realms and societies, determine basic details about magic in your world. Consider which categories of magic are most or least prevalent, how that magic shaped the world's geography, and the nature of the major realms.

*[Page 48]*

#### Categories of Magic

In the worlds of D&D, magic can be categorized in three main ways: by different approaches (arcane or divine), by the schools of magic, and by distinct magical traditions. You might use any of these categories to define the role of magic in your world.

*[Page 48]*

##### Approaches to Magic

The spells of Bards, Sorcerers, Warlocks, and Wizards are commonly called arcane magic. These practitioners rely on direct apprehension of the laws and principles of magic. Wizards study those principles directly, Sorcerers and Bards have a more intuitive understanding, and Warlocks gain that knowledge through their pacts.

The spells of Clerics, Druids, Paladins, and Rangers are called divine magic, though the nature magic of Druids and Rangers is sometimes divided into its own category: primal magic. All these practitioners approach magic mediated by gods, the forces of nature, the sacred weight of a Paladin's oath, or some other divine force.

*[Page 48]*

##### Schools of Magic

The eight schools of magic are Abjuration, Conjuration, Divination, Enchantment, Evocation, Illusion, Necromancy, and Transmutation. These schools represent the underpinnings of all magic in a D&D world. All D&D spells belong to one of these schools.

In your world, spellcasters might experience sensory effects when casting spells of different schools. For example, their scalps might tingle while casting Evocation spells, they might see flashes of events in their peripheral vision while casting Divination spells, or they might smell the phantom scent of grave soil while casting Necromancy spells.

*[Page 48]*

##### Magical Traditions

Spellcasters of any sort might practice a wide variety of magical traditions. Dragons claim to be the originators of magic on some worlds. Some magical traditions rely entirely on rituals (which characters can access through the **Ritual** Caster feat in the *Player's Handbook*). Rune magic (discussed in *Bigby Presents: Glory of the Giants*) was pioneered by giants and represents a distinct magical tradition. In the Forgotten Realms setting, ancient elves developed the tradition of high magic and used it to transform the world. Spellcasters in that setting also developed the practice of Circle magic (detailed in *Forgotten Realms: Heroes of Faerûn*), which allows spellcasters to pool their efforts to cast powerful spells. Magical traditions can also be passed down through family lines, as is the case with Eberron's dragonmarks—magical birthmarks that grant the use of specific spells (as described in *Eberron: Forge of the Artificer*).

*[Page 49]*

#### Magical Geography

Think about the ways magic has shaped the physical landscape of your world. Perhaps a powerful archmage, desiring privacy, used magic to raise mountains around her home. Or a nation governed by powerful mages might cloak the realm in protective magic to block its enemies' magical prying. Your world might boast enormous monuments or strange geographical features created by magic in eons long gone or left behind by the gods in an ancient war.

Magic can have more modest effects too, which nonetheless impact the daily life of your world's residents. For example, in Calimport in the Forgotten Realms, a magical lake inside the city supplies water for residents, while a floating tower serves as headquarters for soldiers who ride flying carpets. The more common magic is in your setting, the more visible its effects should be throughout the world.

*[Page 49]*

#### Magical Realms

As you think about your world's geography, consider the realms within it. Are they all home to powerful spellcasters? Are some more reliant on magic than others?

Your world might include three realms that each rely on a different type of magic. For example, one realm is ruled by wizards who each focus on a particular school of magic. In contrast, another realm is the domain of warlocks who gain their powers from a mysterious, otherworldly entity, while the third realm is ruled by sorcerers who descend from dragons and those who worshipped them. This emphasis on different kinds of spellcasting in each realm gives each a distinct flavor and culture.

On the other hand, you might find it useful to have realms in your world that don't emphasize magic to serve as a contrast to the realms that do. A small realm surrounded by warlike neighbors with large armies of ordinary soldiers, for example, might turn to magic to defend itself and become a haven for mages.

*[Page 49]*

### Attitudes Toward Magic

Once you've determined the types of magic that pervade your world, consider how your world's inhabitants view magic. Do they welcome magic and the potential it represents? Or do they resent and fear magic, seeing it as a force for unexpected change and danger? Do attitudes vary by region?

*[Page 49]*

#### Trust in or Distrust of Magic

A society that has relied on magic for generations has likely grown accustomed to magic's effects on daily life. Such societies' traditions and practices take magic into account. If access to the **Find Familiar** spell is common, for example, public spaces and businesses would be designed to accommodate familiars as well as people.

Alternatively, a society might distrust magic that's not a part of daily life. Perhaps an unfamiliar kind of magic is blamed for a memorable tragedy, leading people to distrust that type of magic. For example, a realm that's been invaded by an army of undead might prohibit necromancy even if the realm encourages other schools of magic.

*[Page 49]*

#### Magical Shortcuts

Magic can often accomplish tasks more quickly and easily than is normally possible. While people in a magic-rich society easily accept the convenience and reliability of magical substitutes for common services, there might be a backlash effect or unusual side effects from folk taking magic for granted.

For example, wealthy nobles might seek out a chef capable of making food "the old-fashioned way." Or a society rife with healing magic might become obsessed with thrill-seeking and dangerous sports.

*[Page 49]*

### Religion

Most D&D worlds have many gods, and a magical society usually has at least one god of magic.

Are there gods of magic in your world? Who are they? What do they expect of their worshippers and from individuals who wield magic? Are they benevolent, antagonistic, or ambivalent toward mortals?

*[Page 49]*

#### Benevolent Magic Gods

You might include a benevolent god of magic in your world. Such a god believes magic should be used for good, improving the lives of ordinary people. They encourage teaching and promoting the use of magic, and aspiring mages are expected to maintain a moral and ethical code that emphasizes selflessness, duty, and charity.

For example, crafters in a world with a benevolent god of magic are encouraged to make Common magic items that benefit others. When adventurers recover lost magic from ancient dungeons, priests hide dangerous magic away while they share useful magic with as many people as possible.

Including a benevolent god of magic in your world doesn't preclude the inclusion of evil mages. Indeed, a benevolent god of magic might be outnumbered or outmatched by evil rivals. This prompts the god of magic or their followers to seek help from the characters.

*[Page 50]*

##### Mystra

Mystra is a benevolent god of magic in the Forgotten Realms setting. Mystra has been destroyed or defeated multiple times, but each time she has returned in a new form with the help of brave adventurers.

*[Page 50]*

#### Antagonistic Magic Gods

A god of magic might spread evil, cruelty, and tyranny through the use of magic. An antagonistic god of magic favors nefarious uses of magic. Such a god's necromancer followers might raise large armies of undead to unleash on the population. Or adherents might use powerful Evocation spells to destroy rival cities and those who live in them. An antagonistic god of magic might rule a realm dedicated to evil and populated with undead, devils or demons, and other bloodthirsty creatures.

*[Page 50]*

##### Iuz and Vecna

The demigod Iuz, described in the *Dungeon Master's Guide*, and *Vecna*, whose schemes to amass ultimate power are described in *Vecna: Eve of Ruin*, are both antagonistic gods who seek conquest through magic. Iuz is primarily a god of deceit and oppression, but his methods involve seeking ancient magic he can use to increase his power. Vecna plots from the shadows and reveals himself only when victory is nigh.

*[Page 50]*

#### Ambivalent Magic Gods

A god of magic might be ambivalent about their followers. Such a deity cares more about whether magic is used than about how it is used. When characters recover lost magical secrets during an adventure, an ambivalent god of magic might want those magical secrets locked away and preserved—not out of a selfish desire to control the information, but out of an intellectual interest in ensuring this knowledge isn't lost again.

*[Page 50]*

##### Mystryl

An ambivalent god of magic might guard the source of all magic or even embody magic. Mystryl, the original god of magic in the Forgotten Realms, embodied magic in this way. When Mystryl died, magic was transformed; spellcasting became unreliable, and spellcasters lost their ability to cast their most powerful spells. Magic stabilized only when the god of magic returned as Mystra.

*[Page 50]*

##### Boccob

An ambivalent god of magic in the Greyhawk setting and patron of the legendary mage *Mordenkainen*, Boccob works to ensure that good and evil forces remain equal.

*[Page 50]*

#### Weaving a Divine Tapestry

A high-magic setting might include multiple gods of magic, each with conflicting goals and methods. No single god is likely to completely satisfy a society ruled by mages where spellcasting is common. Including multiple competing gods of magic gives your players more choices for patron gods for their characters. The conflict between rival gods of magic can create adventures and drive campaigns.

Your world might have different gods for each school of magic (a god of Conjuration, for example, and a god of Transmutation). Or a primary god of magic might be assisted by other gods, each with their own emphasis. For example, Mystra is assisted by Azuth, a god of wizardry who is ambivalent in his outlook and uninterested in the moral and ethical use of magic.

Your world might even have no god focused entirely on magic. In a world where magic suffuses every aspect of life, every god might carry some responsibility for the use of magic as it impacts the rest of their portfolio. A god of war cares not just about weapons and bloodshed, but about the use of magic in battle. A god of crafting might have dominion over making magic items and over artisans who use magic in their trades. And a god of knowledge might govern the academic study of magic often associated with gods of magic. Eberron's pantheon is a good example.

*[Page 50]*

### Technology

Many D&D campaigns assume the technology of a medieval or early renaissance setting. However, the *Dungeon Master's Guide* has rules for modern firearms and futuristic weapons such as **Antimatter Rifle** and **Laser Pistol**. Further examples of futuristic technology can be found in *Quests from the Infinite Staircase*.

When you create your magical world, consider what level of technology is present. Does advanced technology exist in your setting? If it does, what do people think of it? Is technology a replacement for or a supplement to magic?

*[Page 50]*

#### Technological Revolution

To introduce more advanced technology into your world, you can present it as originating in a recent technological revolution. An ancient and magic-focused realm ruled by mages might suddenly discover technological innovation. This could streamline daily life with simple technology, such as long-range communications, and add options for characters who like technology. It could also threaten the realm's rulers and therefore lead to technology being heavily regulated. Threatened rulers might hunt down inventors and destroy their creations. On the flip side, rulers might feel threatened by magic-users, leading these rulers to embrace technology to stave off power-hungry mages.

*[Page 51]*

#### Rival Powers

![In Eberron, magic mimics technology, allowing wonders such as airships. (Art: Andrey Kuzinskiy)](book/AU/030-03-003.eberron-magic.webp)

Alternatively, you can introduce technology to your world by creating a rival realm that relies on technology much like your magical society relies on magic. These two realms could be equivalent in wealth, status, and influence, but what one accomplishes with spells, the other achieves through machines.

Consider whether characters might come from either realm, or if the entire party hails from one society or the other. The first case encourages wildly different characters; some heroes enter battle with the **Magic Missile** and **Fireball** spells prepared, while others rely on their trusty **Laser Pistol** and **Grenade Launcher**.

If all characters come from one of the realms, the other society can serve as a rival and antagonist. If, for example, all your characters are from a magical society bordering a high-technology realm, tension between these two lands runs high and conflict is inevitable. Your campaign might begin with the characters infiltrating their technologically advanced neighbor while concealing their magical skills. There, the characters discover a plan for a mechanical army to invade the party's homeland. To thwart this invasion, the characters need to lead a force of conjured creatures, undead, and other magical warriors.

*[Page 51]*

#### Magic and Technology in Tandem

Magic and technology need not be oppositional forces. You can create a society in which magic and technology operate side by side. In such a realm, bandits are just as likely to cast the **Fire Bolt** spell as they are to draw a **Laser Pistol**. Citizens predict the weather with the **Druidcraft** spell, but they tell time using a clock in the middle of town.

*[Page 52]*

##### Eberron

In a variant of this approach, magic provides the power for technology. Magic is used this way in Eberron. Eberron has airships, trains, and other examples of industrial technology, but these devices are powered by spells, magic items, and Elementals instead of nonmagical batteries and engines. Instantaneous, long-distance communication is possible in Eberron through **Sending Stones** staffed by members of a dragonmarked house; the result is akin to a magical telegraph network. Similarly, the residents of your society might have access to magic items that duplicate the effects of phones, automobiles, and similar modern conveniences.

*[Page 52]*

### Laws

Even a novice spellcaster, able to cast nothing more than a cantrip, can use their magic to kill. Powerful mages can secretly observe individuals from afar, probe the thoughts of others, or control minds. It stands to reason that magical societies would impose laws to restrict the use of invasive, coercive, and destructive magic.

When creating your magical world, consider how the law restricts the use of magic, if at all. Are mages punished only when their magic hurts others? Are certain spells always restricted, or does anything go?

*[Page 52]*

#### Outlawed Magic

The simplest magic law is the strictest: a ban on all magic. Laws like this come about because a society has a long history of conflict with magical enemies, because magic is blamed for environmental or social ills, or because of simple superstition. Power-hungry mages who run a society might outlaw the use of magic by anyone other than the ruling class.

*[Page 52]*

##### Vilhon Reach

Several regions of the Vilhon Reach in the Forgotten Realms curtail or ban magic, particularly magic used to charm or injure others.

*[Page 52]*

#### Focusing on the Crime

Some magical societies consider laws against the use of magic unnecessary when existing laws already prohibit the harmful effects of magic. After all, murder is murder, whether the victim was killed with a sword, a spell, or the killer's bare hands.

For example, a large city like Greyhawk or Waterdeep might have laws against theft and laws that prohibit spying on a person in their home. An individual caught performing such acts would be guilty of a crime whether they cast the **Invisibility** spell or used nonmagical means.

Magic might count as an aggravating factor during sentencing, however. An individual found guilty of murder who killed the victim with the **Finger of Death** spell might be given a harsher sentence by a judge.

*[Page 52]*

#### Frontier Justice

In remote regions that lack effective law enforcement, individuals and communities decide how magic and mages are treated. Residents might be suspicious of magic and intolerant of public spellcasting. Or they might become antagonistic toward mages who use magic in a way the residents don't like. As a third option, a single law enforcement official, such as a sheriff or marshal, might decide which mages are allowed to practice their craft undisturbed and which are driven out of town.

*[Page 52]*

##### Ten-Towns

In the remote settlements of Ten-Towns in the Forgotten Realms, an innkeeper might hang up a "No Spells Allowed" sign and hire a bouncer or two to enforce this rule. Putting up such a sign is the innkeeper's right, but the town guard (if there is a town guard) is under no obligation to enforce it.

*[Page 52]*

### Commerce

How magic intertwines with commerce in your world is a key part of how your characters interact with it.

How easily can your characters find magic item shops or mages to purchase spellcasting services from? How accessible are magic items of higher rarity, such as Legendary magic items or Artifacts? Do mages' guilds regulate the accessibility of magic?

*[Page 52]*

#### Magic Item Shops

![Members of House Deneith in Eberron work as paid, magic-wielding bodyguards. (Art: Eilene Cherie)](book/AU/031-03-004.house-deneith.webp)

While **Spell Scroll** and **Potion of Healing** are included in gear a character can buy in the *Player's Handbook*, the *Dungeon Master's Guide* leaves it up to the DM to decide what magic items are available to purchase in a campaign. A variety of magic item shops might be common in your world.

If you want Rare or rarer magic items to feel special, treat them like antiquities or fine art. A magic item shop in such a setting could be owned and operated by a single person who has deep knowledge of magical lore and contacts all over the world. When an adventurer asks for a specific magic item, the shop owner can procure it eventually, but the search takes time and leads to an adventure.

If magic items are more common in your world, a magic item shop in a city might look more like a pawnshop. All kinds of magic items would line the shelves, including duplicates of especially useful items such as **Bag of Holding**. The owner might be assisted by several individuals who specialize in security and deterring theft.

*[Page 53]*

#### Magical Businesses

While any mage can sell their spellcasting services, your magical society might be filled with mages who use magic to earn a living in more interesting ways. The *Player's Handbook* presents availability and cost details for spellcasting services, but you can alter this availability as best suits your world.

For example, a novice spellcaster who can cast the **Prestidigitation** spell could operate a laundry service, quickly and easily cleaning clothes. A chef could use the same spell to flavor food for an hour. Cantrips such as **Elementalism**, **Guidance**, **Mending**, and **Spare the Dying** are also well suited for a humble but reliable magical career.

Magical businesses could take dramatic forms in your setting. Eberron is full of examples, from the magical artificers of House Cannith to the magical bodyguards of House Deneith.

*[Page 53]*

#### Mages' Guilds

A mages' guild is an alliance of mages who run a robust magical business in a city. Such guilds are a staple of fantasy roleplaying games, especially campaigns with roots in D&D's earliest editions and settings, such as Greyhawk.

*[Page 53]*

##### Guild Practices

A successful mages' guild works hand in hand with the local law enforcement. Typically, local laws don't prohibit spellcasting or making magic items, but they prohibit those who don't belong to the guild from making a profit by providing services that fall under the guild's exclusive concerns.

The local law enforcement typically includes members who are mages—and members of the guild. Both the guild and law enforcement tend to spend considerable time and effort monitoring mages in the city, enforcing guild rules and looking for mages avoiding guild membership.

*[Page 54]*

##### Membership

Mages in a guild enjoy a monopoly on magical commerce. They expect to be paid an agreed-on amount of money for their services. Further, the guild is an excellent place for networking with other mages, and it probably operates a guildhall that includes a library of magical tomes and spellbooks as well as dining facilities, sleeping areas, and more. A guildhall could serve as an excellent base of operations for a group of characters connected to the guild.

Guild members are expected to pay dues and obey the guild's rules. In this way, a mages' guild in your world could serve as your characters' employer—as well as their debt holder.

*[Page 54]*

##### Rank

Membership in the guild might be structured into three or more ranks. While members of the lowest rank have no influence in the guild and are expected to assist senior members for little or no pay, high-ranking members enjoy a wealthy lifestyle, authority in the guild, and the assistance of lower-ranking members.

*[Page 54]*

##### Campaign Role

A mages' guild can serve as a group patron for characters or as an adversary that must be overcome. As a patron, the guild offers access to spellbooks, a comfortable refuge from danger, and magical allies knowledgeable on numerous subjects.

But a mages' guild might instead be an oppressive organization that ruthlessly exploits its monopoly and harasses nonmembers. Your campaign could focus on ousting the guild's leaders and replacing them with egalitarian leadership.

*[Page 54]*

### Magical Armies

![A War Wizard of Cormyr casts a powerful Evocation spell on the battlefield. (Art: Swatches Studio)](book/AU/032-03-005.war-wizard-of-cormyr.webp)

Fantasy realms raise and maintain armies to defend their territories. A society steeped in magic might establish magical armies to complement its regular troops. What benefits do magical armies provide, and what forms do they take?

*[Page 54]*

#### War Wizards

A magical army might boast mages in its ranks, either mixed in among ordinary soldiers or forming elite units composed entirely of spellcasters. The War Wizards of Cormyr in the Forgotten Realms is an archetypal example of this kind of elite unit.

War wizards defy mage stereotypes. They often wear armor and wield martial weapons, resembling Eldritch Knights. Alternatively, a war wizard might wear simple robes and wield a staff but focus on damaging spells. These mages function as mobile artillery units, protected by martial soldiers. **Spellguard** (see **chapter 6**) might feature prominently in such an army.

*[Page 54]*

#### Magical Minions

A magical army might supplement or even replace ordinary soldiers with magical minions. During the Last War, Eberron's armies extensively used magical minions, with the nation of Karrnath fielding armies of zombies and skeletons while Cyre and other nations used constructs. Outside Eberron, other magical realms fill their armies with conjured creatures or elementals summoned from the Inner Planes.

Fielding magical minions allows commanders and martial soldiers to remain safely behind the battle line while the minions participate in combat. A realm heavily dependent on magical minions might enter war more quickly than other realms and take more risks on the battlefield, brushing off the loss of magical minions as insignificant since they're easily replaced.

*[Page 55]*

#### Magical Fortifications

A traditional castle isn't much of a defense against mages who can cast spells such as **Fly**, **Passwall**, and **Teleport**. Fortunately, a realm with magical resources has many magical ways to defend itself. Defensive measures such as the **Wall of Force**, **Globe of Invulnerability**, and **Antimagic Field** spells cover a small area for a limited time and are useful in emergencies. But options such as the **Forbiddance** and **Guards and Wards** spells last much longer and can provide layers of magical defenses. Entrances can be protected with the **Glyph of Warding** spell.

A fortification might be protected by magical defenders that negate an enemy's advantages. For example, flying assailants could be countered with flying defenders, such as soldiers mounted on griffins, hippogriffs, or pegasi.

*[Page 55]*

### Mastering Magic

![In your world, magical instruction might involve hundreds of students learning from masters in an arena such as this one. (Art: Sylvain Sarrailh)](book/AU/033-03-006.magical-arena.webp)

If magic suffuses your world, consider how its inhabitants learn their magical craft. Is magic laboriously taught by an elderly expert to a single apprentice? Do aspiring mages enroll at an academy where they're taught alongside others? Or do novice spellcasters learn another way?

The forms of magical instruction can be described in four ways: solitary study, apprenticeship, academic instruction, or cultural immersion, where magical instruction is readily available to all. Most magical societies rely on a single type of magical instruction. You might decide, however, that different kinds of instruction are prevalent in different parts of the world. For example, a magical realm might boast a magical academy with hundreds of students, but in the realm's capital, magic might be so common that everyone knows a spell or two thanks to the cultural prevalence of magic in everyday life.

*[Page 55]*

#### Solitary Study

When magic is learned through solitary study, each mage understands magic slightly differently. For example, one mage might conceive of magic as influenced by the stars and planets, while another conceptualizes magic as an imperceptible force that connects all things. Both mages can cast spells of equivalent strength and effect, but their understanding of the underlying origin of magic varies.

*[Page 56]*

##### Warlocks

In a society that relies on solitary study, every mage is self-taught. Warlocks, who cobble together magical power from pacts and little-understood scraps of lore, are the most common kind of spellcaster in these societies. Wizards, who traditionally rely on learning from others, might be rare.

*[Page 56]*

#### Apprenticeship

When magic is learned through apprenticeship, magical instruction is limited to one-on-one relationships between an expert and a novice. An experienced mage might have many apprentices in a lifetime but teach only one at a time. As a result, there are always more candidates for apprenticeship than mentors, and competition among potential apprentices might be intense. Experienced mages might screen candidates or develop elaborate tests for prospective apprentices, measuring their intelligence, loyalty, magical acumen, or ethical code. These tests might serve as low-level adventures in your world.

*[Page 56]*

##### Campaigns

In a campaign focused on apprenticeship, the mentor becomes unusually important. Craft interesting mentors your players will enjoy interacting with. The archetypal mentor is enigmatic and seemingly omnipotent, but they can also be whimsical, grouchy, or absent-minded.

In addition to creating a memorable mentor, consider when and how your characters' apprenticeships end. Is a level 1 Wizard finished with their apprenticeship? Or do they remain an apprentice until level 3 or later? How does the mentor decide when the student has learned enough?

*[Page 56]*

##### Sorcerers

Sorcerers flourish in societies focused on apprenticeship. Many sorcerers inherit their powers from a magical ancestor, or their powers might be shared among members of their family. These family connections make it easier to secure a mentor, and the family might have well-established traditions around recognizing, controlling, and mastering magic.

*[Page 57]*

#### Academic Instruction

In a long-established magical society, the myriad magical philosophies taught by individual mentors eventually coalesce around the most successful teachers. When these teachers pool their resources to form a magical academy, large numbers of students can benefit from academic instruction. Styles of magical academies are further detailed in "**Magical Academies**" below.

*[Page 57]*

##### Wizards

Wizards flourish in a magical society with academic instruction. Dutifully showing up to school every day, books in hand, Wizards learn new spells in a classroom environment. During off hours, they practice magic, pursue magical interests on their own, and get into the sort of trouble students always find.

*[Page 57]*

#### Cultural Immersion

Magic in your society might be so well understood and so widespread that aspiring mages can learn basic magic from everyday life. In such a society, books of magical instruction might be found at any bookstore or library. Everyone might know a spell or two, and learning a new one might be as easy as sitting down for a few hours with a friend and exchanging techniques. When magic is widely understood and a part of everyday life, you don't need to create magical academies or mentor figures to explain why characters know basic magic.

*[Page 57]*

##### Bards

Bards—whose magic takes the form of music, dance, and other popular art forms—flourish in a society where magic is popular. To Bards, learning new magic might be as simple as overhearing a catchy tune in a tavern and reworking it for their own purposes.

*[Page 57]*

#### Magical Academies

The magical academies in the worlds of D&D make useful models for your world-building. Consider the following when creating your magical academy.

![Magical academies sometimes have their own sports, such as the game of Mage Tower at Strixhaven. (Art: Swatches Studio)](book/AU/034-03-007.magical-academies.webp)

*[Page 57]*

##### Everything Under One Roof

The most common form of magical academy is a single place where several kinds of magic are taught. For example, Strixhaven, detailed in *Strixhaven: A Curriculum of Chaos*, is a magical academy where five different campuses teach opposing magic philosophies. Similarly, the Tower of High Sorcery in the Dragonlance setting, described in *Dragonlance: Shadow of the Dragon Queen*, teaches that setting's three primary orders of magic.

An academy of this sort is home to mages with different philosophies, goals, and methods. These individuals are united around the higher goal of teaching magic to the next generation of students. A student at one of these academies has access to a wide variety of spells, magical traditions, and instructors. Because students come from far away, they benefit from interacting with people from all over the world.

This kind of academy provides players with a lot of character creation options. Characters of any background, species, class, or subclass might come together to study magic and are sent on adventures by teachers with competing agendas. A magical academy is an example of an esoteric institution group patron, detailed later in this chapter.

*[Page 57]*

##### Rival Academies

A realm with a large population of aspiring mages might have multiple magical academies that each specialize in a different form of magic.

An academy of this sort might focus on a single kind of magic: for instance, magic from the Conjuration school, or bladesinging, an ancient elven technique of magical combat. Rivalries between such academies take forms ranging from athletic competitions to pranks, clandestine raids, or even physical violence.

Because all students at one such academy study a single kind of magic, the academy is less likely to function as the home base for your campaign. Instead, the rival academies likely feature in the backstories of characters who wield magic, serving as the source of the characters' early magical training. However, you might create a campaign in which graduates of these rival academies are brought together by a menace that threatens all the academies or even the entire realm.

> *[Page 57]*
>
> **Magical Academies in the Real World**
>
>
> In our world, old traditions speak of schools where eager students went to learn magic. Transylvanian legend describes the Scholomance, a school for sorcery where the devil taught ten students at a time. At the end of their term of instruction, nine students were allowed to depart, but the devil claimed the tenth. Other examples of magical academies in folklore include the Cave of Salamanca in Spain and Domdaniel, a magical school first mentioned in a French continuation of The Arabian Nights.

## Magical Group Patrons

Whether your heroes wish to advance the agenda of a famous mage or seek access to a magical academy's ample resources, a group patron with significant magical prowess can create the cohesion that holds a campaign together and help your game feel infused with magic.

This section presents six types of magical group patrons around which you can create campaigns, as summarized on the Magical Patron Options table.

Each sample patron includes examples from the D&D multiverse and outlines for patronage campaigns. You can incorporate these into your game or use them as inspiration for your own magical group patron.

This section also explores the option of your group's patron hiding their identity for reasons benevolent or malign, which can be used with any of the group patrons that follow.

*[Page 58]*
**Table: Magical Patron Options**

| Category | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Archmage** | A powerful, often famous mage with clear goals that span the multiverse |
| **Esoteric Institution** | An organization dedicated to education, research, or containing dangerous magic |
| **Magical Entity** | A non-Humanoid mage or magical creature with a mysterious, timeless outlook |
| **Political Faction** | A group of powerful, like-minded mages deeply invested in political goals |
| **Trickster** | A capricious individual with inscrutable motives and a malevolent or whimsical nature |
| **Wizards' Circle** | An alliance of mages focused on pursuing a difficult goal or resolving a problem or threat |

*[Page 58]*

### How Patrons Work

No matter their details, group patrons provide adventurers with some common benefits.

*[Page 58]*

#### Group Assistance

Having a group patron gives an adventuring group a common purpose, which inspires better coordination in the form of guidance and encouragement. As a result of this connection, each member of your party can grant **Advantage** on a **D20 Test** made by another member of the party. To grant **Advantage** in this way, you and your target must be able to see or hear each other, and neither can have the **Incapacitated** condition. After granting **Advantage** in this way, you can't do so again until you finish a **Long Rest**.

*[Page 58]*

#### Perks

Group patrons offer characters perks ranging from a steady wage to access to libraries and magical resources to unexpected allies. Specific perks are presented in the description of each group patron.

*[Page 58]*

### Option: Make Your Patron Mysterious

Concealing the true identity of your group patron can be a fun campaign linchpin, especially if your game involves magic-themed intrigue. Any of the magical group patrons described in this chapter might have reasons to stay anonymous. If you use a mysterious patron, create a strong reason the patron is hiding their identity.

*[Page 58]*

#### High-Profile Patron

A group patron might remain anonymous because they're incredibly powerful, famous or infamous, or otherwise high profile, and their identity being known would undermine the characters' goals or ability to operate clandestinely. Or the patron might have reasons they wish to remain publicly unassociated with the characters. For example, perhaps the patron is a powerful political figure who can't publicly take sides in a civil dispute but wants the characters to side with freedom fighters over greedy merchants.

*[Page 58]*

#### Ulterior Motives

![Powerful patrons are eager to sponsor young heroes, but their intent is sometimes unclear. (Art: Swatches Studio)](book/AU/035-03-008.powerful-patrons.webp)

On the other hand, a patron might hide their identity because their motives are malevolent. The patron might try to trick the characters into performing deeds that destabilize a fragile peace or empower an evil overlord. Or a patron might remain anonymous because the characters would never knowingly work for them, especially if the patron is nefarious and the heroes are upstanding.

> *[Page 58]*
>
> > Kindness promotes peace and good, but the practicalities of the world require coin. A patron who supplies the latter is useful; a patron who also believes in you is invaluable.
> > — *Alustriel*

*[Page 59]*

#### Concealing Their Identity

Mysterious magical patrons don't show their true faces to the characters, communicating via anonymous letters, prearranged signals, and networks of underlings similarly unaware of the patron's identity. A mysterious patron might refer to themself with a code name that fits the nature of the missions they ask the characters to undertake. In this case, the characters can't gain benefits associated with the patron's identity, such as the archmage's and esoteric institution's Documentation perks or the magical entity's Name Drop perk. Instead, the characters must find ways other than invoking their patron's name to achieve their goals.

*[Page 59]*

##### Using Magic

Since magical patrons are typically powerful mages, they use high-level spell effects to ensure the characters don't discover their identity. A patron might repeatedly use the **Programmed Illusion** spell to convey information to the characters via fictional, illusory agents. If a patron must interact with a character, the patron might use the **Seeming** spell to significantly alter their appearance. Similarly, a patron might use the **Mirage Arcane** spell to conceal their lair's true location behind an illusory waterfall or trees. As your characters gain power, ensure their patron uses magic that the characters are unlikely to dispel.

*[Page 59]*

##### Keeping the Characters on Board

If you use a mysterious group patron in your game, it's important for the characters to remain convinced that the patron's goals align with theirs. Attribute to the patron's code name deeds that are benevolent or helpful to the characters. Or ensure the patron compensates the characters well for their work, giving the characters a practical reason to continue working for them.

*[Page 59]*

#### The Big Reveal

Eventually, the novelty of being sponsored by a mysterious group patron will wear thin. The characters might lose interest in working for someone unwilling to reveal their identity. They might begin to distrust or become frustrated with the patron.

When the time is right, you might reveal the patron's identity. You can do so suddenly, with the patron meeting the characters in person to thank them for their work, or more subtly.

*[Page 59]*

##### Dropping Hints

As the characters complete more of the patron's missions, the patron might begin to drop—intentionally or unintentionally—coded hints about their identity. Investigating these clues can become a fun side quest for the characters.

*[Page 60]*

##### Benevolent Patron

Once the characters have solved the clues, the patron might appear in person to congratulate them on discovering the secret. The patron might have intended to reveal themself once the characters proved themselves worthy through solving the clues, or the characters might have preempted the patron's plans. Either way, this gradual approach provides satisfaction to characters eager to learn their patron's identity.

*[Page 60]*

##### Malevolent Patron

If the patron's motives are malicious, the characters might react with horror to their patron's identity. In this case, providing a satisfying conclusion to your campaign involves the characters confirming their patron's identity and formulating a plan for dealing with them. That might involve leaving the patron's employ abruptly, turning the tables on the patron to discredit them, confronting the patron in combat, or another option.

Follow your players' lead in how to wrap up the campaign. You might decide that the patron anticipated the characters' realization, but keep in mind that defeating this duplicitous patron allows a satisfying conclusion.

*[Page 60]*

### Archmage

As incredibly powerful spellcasters, archmages are capable of impressive deeds. Beyond slaying dragons or destroying undead, archmages have saved (and ruled!) entire cities, driven back armies, defeated the forces of the *Nine Hells* or the *Abyss*, and traveled in time. The mightiest archmages achieve magical mastery over the physical world. Some no longer age, spending centuries amassing unfathomable magical knowledge and prowess and creating spells they name after themselves.

With archmages' great learning and magical capabilities comes even greater willpower. Standing among the multiverse's strongest personalities, archmages personify stubbornness (even among the best) and self-centeredness (prominent among the worst). This unyielding nature translates into a voracious drive, whether it's to protect the weak, combat the wicked, amass the most knowledge, or take the biggest risks.

*[Page 60]*

#### Archmage Motivations

**Archmage** might patronize a group for the following reasons:

1. **Emissaries Are Needed** **Archmage** amass allies and commune with powerful leaders during their travels throughout the multiverse. An archmage hires the characters to travel to these allies and request their aid—or aid those allies—in the archmage's pursuit of friendly relations or magical research.
2. **Evil Is Rising** Benevolent archmages recognize the sheer power of unchecked evil and are sensitive to such forces amassing strength or plotting terrible deeds. An archmage who discovers complex and wicked schemes hires the characters to complete tasks that undermine those schemes.
3. **Lieutenants Are Required** An archmage who identifies numerous threats plaguing a certain area hires the characters to face a host of lesser threats so the archmage can focus on powerful villainous leaders.

*[Page 60]*

#### Archmage Patrons

The following characters from the D&D multiverse could serve as archmage group patrons:

![Lady Alustriel Silverhand is a powerful archmage who patronizes groups in the Forgotten Realms and beyond. (Art: Liliia Abdullina)](book/AU/036-03-009.alustriel-silverhand.webp)

1. **Alustriel Silverhand** One of the most powerful mages in the Forgotten Realms setting, Alustriel has been a proponent of good and enemy of evil for centuries. Alustriel is an immortal human daughter of Mystra, a god of magic, and ruled the city of Silverymoon for centuries. Alustriel maintains safe houses scattered across the planes for her allies as they promote compassion throughout the multiverse.
2. **Vajra Safahr** Chief mage of Waterdeep in the Forgotten Realms setting, the human Vajra is the youngest head in the history of Blackstaff Academy, a school for mages. While Vajra fends off threats to her city and her university, she also faces political conflicts from those who would unseat her from her place of prestige.
3. **Yolande** Known as the Faerie Queen, Yolande is the benevolent and beloved elf monarch of the nation of Celene in the world of Greyhawk. She is a mighty wizard who protects her nation against the incursion of Elemental Evil forces as well as threats against the Feywild and the Material Plane.

*[Page 60]*

#### Creating Your Own Archmage

Instead of using one of the archmages described above, you can create an archmage to serve as a group patron for your characters. You might start by choosing one of the eight archmage stat blocks in chapter 6, each of which specializes in one school of magic, and add personality details as you see fit.

When creating an archmage, consider how close to the traditional archetype you want your archmage to be. A traditional archmage is incredibly powerful and eccentric. They're easily distracted because they're nurturing layers of complicated schemes. Your archmage could be very different: plain spoken, attentive to the characters, not as powerful as their reputation would suggest—or far more so.

When you create an archmage for your campaign, you can roll on or choose from the Archmage Type table to determine details of the archmage's persona.

| 2 | Disguised Sage. The archmage disguises themself as a humble apprentice of limited magical ability, while their loud and boastful student pretends to be the archmage. The two keep their true identities secret from the party. |

#### Archmage Patron Perks

Characters in a party whose patron is an archmage gain the following perks.

*[Page 62]*

##### Compensation

**Archmage** amass considerable wealth during their adventures and travels, and archmage patrons generously compensate their underlings. The nature of your work for an archmage influences how you're paid. On average, the archmage pays each member of your group 2 GP per day (enough to sustain a *Comfortable* lifestyle). Or you might be paid (at least 300 GP) each time you complete a task the archmage assigns.

*[Page 62]*

##### Documentation

Each member of your group has identification noting their affiliation with the archmage, which carries clout in areas where the archmage is known. If the archmage is the ruler of a city or nation, the documentation grants the characters appropriate authority in that realm.

*[Page 62]*

##### Magical Resources

**Archmage** collect untold magic items and magical curiosities, though these collections are often scattered among far-flung sanctums. An archmage can give you any Common or Uncommon magic item in the *Dungeon Master's Guide*, as long as possession of the item would help your group's tasks. When your group reaches level 11, the archmage can retrieve one Rare and one Very Rare magic item in between the group's tasks and award it to you as additional compensation. When your group reaches level 17, the archmage can retrieve one Rare, Very Rare, or Legendary magic item in between tasks and award it to you as additional compensation.

*[Page 62]*

##### Spellcasting

**Archmage** can use their spellpower to help your missions. An archmage can cast any level 1 or lower spell for you at no cost, as long as the spell's effects would aid your work. The archmage can cast any level 2–8 spell for you at half the usual cost.

#### Archmage Contacts

An archmage group patron might prioritize working with the characters, meeting with the party directly to deliver missions and collect reports. In this case, the archmage becomes a significant recurring character in your campaign, so give the archmage a manner of speaking or another feature that makes them distinctive. For example, the archmage might talk to their snake familiar, wear outrageously garish outfits, or hum odd tunes when distracted.

But archmages have a lot to do, and the patron might send a representative in their place. Roll on or choose from the Archmage Contact table to determine how the archmage interacts with the characters.

#### An Archmage Campaign

A campaign driven by an archmage patron might unfold according to this outline:

1. **Levels 1–4** As the characters begin their adventuring career, the archmage watches them from afar, monitoring their activities and subtly directing their attention to the groundlings (see **chapter 6**) that a wicked rival archmage commands.
2. **Levels 5–10** The archmage reveals themself to the characters and hires the characters to investigate the rival archmage, whose schemes threaten the city or surrounding realm. You can use the stat block for one of the archmages in chapter 6 for this rival, whom the party confronts in a climactic battle.
3. **Levels 11–16** The archmage patron sends the characters all over the world to gather magical secrets from creatures that hoard knowledge; adult dragons, devils, and a **Demilich** make suitable antagonists.
4. **Levels 17–20** The archmage patron retires. The characters are now obliged to deal with the archmage's enemies and rivals while exploring the archmage's extraplanar lair.

### Esoteric Institution

An esoteric institution might be a magical academy, an order of monks researching lost magical techniques, or any other organization focused on the study, preservation, and use of magical lore. Institutions usually maintain a base of operations such as a campus, monastery, or remote fortress. The institution might have a large staff including administrators, teachers, groundskeepers, and guards. In addition to an excellent library, it probably has a secure vault for storing dangerous magic items, and more than one Artifact might be housed there.

Esoteric institutions have a long history that stretches back generations. In that time, an institution has made enemies and collected magic items that have been stored away and forgotten. The institution's headquarters might have secret chambers protected by puzzles and traps. Despite its reclusive or insular nature, the institution monitors the world beyond its walls. When it finds magic being used for evil or learns of long-forgotten lore to be recovered, it sends adventurers on missions.

![The Tower of High Sorcery in the world of Dragonlance charges heroes with protecting magical secrets from a destructive draconic army. (Art: Swatches Studio)](book/AU/038-03-011.tower-of-high-sorcery.webp)

*[Page 64]*

#### Esoteric Institution Motivations

An esoteric institution might patronize a group for the following reasons:

1. **The Characters Are Students** The characters are students at an academy such as the Tower of High Sorcery in the Dragonlance setting or Strixhaven University and are sent on missions as part of their studies. The institution doesn't intentionally send the characters on dangerous missions, and it sends rescue teams if the characters get into trouble while pursuing goals tied to the academy.
2. **The Institution Needs Field Agents** An esoteric institution includes sages, scholars, and researchers, but few warriors and explorers. The institution wants to hire the characters for missions deemed too dangerous for its own staff, such as rescuing students in peril.
3. **The Characters Are Specialists** An esoteric institution seeks out the characters because the characters are experts in a particular field relevant to a current problem or interest. For example, if the characters recently defeated a powerful vampire, the institution hires them to recover that vampire's hidden library of magical tomes.

*[Page 64]*

#### Esoteric Institution Patrons

The following organizations from the D&D multiverse could serve as esoteric institution group patrons:

1. **Order of the Guardians** The Order of the Guardians is a group of ascetics who gather, sequester, and protect dangerous magic in the Ravenloft setting. Its members maintain several hidden and well-protected monasteries, but the largest is Watchers' Stronghold in the Mistlands, a piece of the disintegrating domain of Darkon. The order is stretched thin, outnumbered and outmatched by the horrors found in the *Domains of Dread*, and it's quick to hire adventurers willing to venture into dangerous sites to recover powerful items or spells so they can't be used for evil. More information on the Order of the Guardians and Darkon can be found in *Ravenloft: The Horrors Within*.
2. **Thebestys** In the Outlands of the Planescape setting, the walled city of Thebestys is home to an ibis-headed, many-named god of learning. This god maintains a great library at the city's center. The library enjoys a reputation as a matchless repository of knowledge. The ibis-headed god employs the characters as agents who travel through the city of *Sigil* and the planes to find books and bring them back to the library for copying and collection. Thebestys and the Outlands are detailed in *Planescape: Adventures in the Multiverse*.
3. **Tower of High Sorcery** In the Dragonlance setting, there were once five Towers of High Sorcery, where mages were trained. Due to disaster and tragedy, only one remains. There, spellcasters study magic in traditions tied to the world of Krynn's three moons. Characters in this setting serve the mages of the tower as teachers, students, or defenders, and they are dispatched to locations across the world to preserve magical secrets from the destructive draconian army. You can find more information on the Tower of High Sorcery in *Dragonlance: Shadow of the Dragon Queen*.

*[Page 64]*

#### Creating Your Own Esoteric Institution

Instead of using one of the esoteric institutions described above, you can create an institution to serve as a group patron for your characters. This gives you an opportunity to customize the institution to appeal to your players and to reflect the characters they've created. For example, if your players have made wilderness-oriented characters such as Barbarians, Druids, and Rangers, the institution you create is likely focused on preserving nature. If you want tension between the characters and the institution, the leaders of the institution might be of different alignments than the characters.

When you create an esoteric institution for your campaign, roll on or choose from the Esoteric Institution Type table to inspire the institution's details.

#### Esoteric Institution Patron Perks

Characters in a party whose patron is an esoteric institution gain the following perks.

*[Page 65]*

##### Compensation

The institution pays for the work you do on its behalf. The nature of your employment influences your compensation. On average, the institution pays each member of your group 1 GP per day (or enough to sustain a *Modest* lifestyle). Or you might be paid a bounty (at least 250 GP) for each magic item or piece of lore you bring back from your adventures and give to the institution.

*[Page 65]*

##### Documentation

Each member of your group has identification noting their affiliation with the institution, which carries clout in areas where the institution is known. For magical academies such as the Tower of High Sorcery or Strixhaven, this is a letter on official stationery marked with the academy's seal, but for secretive organizations like the Order of the Guardians, the documentation takes the form of a signet ring or brooch marked with a discreet symbol.

*[Page 65]*

##### Esoteric Research

Esoteric institutions include expansive libraries specializing in magical knowledge. When you take a *Bastion* turn, you can (as a group) issue the Research order to the institution as if it were a **Library** facility in your Bastion. The topic chosen must be related to magic in some way; at the DM's discretion, the institution can research topics related to the planes as well. Once you reach level 13, you instead can issue the Research order to the institution as if it were an **Archive**.

*[Page 65]*

##### Scroll and Spellbook Access

You have access to the institution's library of **Spell Scroll** and spellbooks, as well as supplies for copying spells. You can buy any **Spell Scroll** from the institution for half its usual value, as indicated in the *Dungeon Master's Guide*. You can find any spell from the Player's Handbook in the library, and the institution pays half the GP cost of copying the spell.

*[Page 65]*

##### Training

You have access to continuing education at no cost. You can spend 30 days in classes to gain one of the following benefits of your choice:

1. **Skill** You gain proficiency in one of these skills of your choice: **Arcana**, **History**, **Nature**, or **Religion**. You can gain proficiency in only one skill in this way.
2. **Tool** You gain proficiency with a tool of your choice.
3. **Language** You learn a language of your choice.

*[Page 65]*

Think carefully about the contact's personality, because your players will interact with them often. The contact doesn't have to stay the same throughout the campaign; the institution might assign a new contact every mission or only in extreme circumstances.

#### An Esoteric Institution Campaign

A campaign driven by an esoteric institution patron might unfold according to this outline:

1. **Levels 1–4** The characters begin as students at an esoteric institution where they study magic. Their early adventures involve exploring the institution (overcoming animated objects and other guardians) and the surrounding region (perhaps populated by modrons, myconids, or undead). As part of their graduation exam, the characters are tasked to defeat a **Bulette** that threatens a nearby village.
2. **Levels 5–10** Now graduates of the institution, the characters are sent on missions to dangerous, far-off places where magical lore is hidden or being used for evil. They battle cultists of an evil god of magic and infiltrate the lair of a young dragon or a nest of **Mind Flayer**.
3. **Levels 11–16** Eventually the characters are offered senior positions at the institution, where they become teachers of a new generation of pupils. They must defend the institution against enemies such as **Arcanaloth**, **Rakshasa**, and a **Vampire Umbral Lord**.
4. **Levels 17–20** At the climax of the campaign, a traitorous member of the esoteric institution steals an item the party recovered earlier and tries to use it to attain vast power or simple vengeance. The traitor is being manipulated by a **Pit Fiend**, and only the characters can stop them.

### Magical Entity

A magical entity is a powerful creature of mysterious or otherworldly origin. The entity might openly rule the realm in which they live, or they might be a mysterious figure that manipulates events from behind the scenes. Regardless, the magical entity is old, extremely knowledgeable, and frightening.

The magical entity is surrounded by legends and superstition. Villagers and townsfolk in the region know the entity by name. Most magical entities are feared as monsters, even if they're benevolent, though some are revered as honorable creatures that protect the realm.

*[Page 66]*

#### Magical Entity Motivations

Magical entities might patronize a group for the following reasons:

1. **Bound to a Place** The magical entity is bound to a particular location and can't leave. While supremely powerful and all but omniscient in their own realm, the being needs the characters to accomplish objectives beyond the entity's reach.
2. **Like Recognizes Like** The magical entity sees something in the characters that reminds the entity of themself. A good patron likely admires heroes who are kind and just, while an evil patron might be impressed by characters they perceive as proud and cruel.
3. **Too Famous to Travel** The magical entity is so well known—and feared—that they can't travel without attracting unwanted attention and alerting their enemies. The entity needs agents who can avoid notice.

*[Page 66]*

#### Magical Entity Patrons

![Bahamut, a patron of good dragons, enlists heroes to fight against terrible evils. (Art: Loïc Canavaggia)](book/AU/039-03-012.bahamut.webp)

The following beings from the D&D multiverse could serve as magical entity patrons:

1. **Baba Yaga** An arch-hag known as the Mother of All Witches, Baba Yaga travels the multiverse in her chicken-legged hut, pursuing inscrutable goals. She's also the adoptive mother of the famous witch *Tasha*. Baba Yaga might hire characters of all alignments to seek esoteric magical knowledge or to act as her emissaries in formal political environments. Her goals and motivations are mysterious, but she offers great power to those she favors.
2. **Bahamut** *Bahamut* is a god of good in numerous settings and is like a king to metallic dragons. He appears to mortals in various guises, often accompanied by seven yellow canaries that are gold dragons in disguise. In the world of Dragonlance, he has appeared as Fizban the Fabulous, a bumbling mage whose dramatic "accidents" often have hidden purposes. Bahamut is an excellent patron for good-aligned groups; he's unlikely to work with evil characters unless he thinks they can be convinced to change their ways. He's likely to conceal his true nature from the characters.
3. **Strahd von Zarovich** Trapped in the domain of Barovia, the vampire *Strahd* is described in *Ravenloft: The Horrors Within* and *Curse of Strahd*. Strahd wields supreme power in his homeland, but he can't leave it. He needs the characters to be his eyes and ears outside Barovia. Supremely egotistical, Strahd finds the idea of concealing his true identity entertaining for a while, but once the party has done a task or two for him, the mask comes off and the vampire reveals himself. Knowingly working with Strahd for an extended time likely won't appeal to most heroic parties, though Strahd could make a great ongoing patron for amoral adventurers.

Even if your magical entity is frightening, strange, or otherworldly, that doesn't mean the entity must be evil. Creatures commonly associated with evil deeds—such as chromatic dragons or undead—might have a change of heart and shift to a neutral or good alignment. But even a good magical entity can come across as weird, remote, or unsettling. Ordinary folk flee from the entity, but the characters are brave enough to interact with them.

##### Compensation

The magical entity pays for the work you do on their behalf. For every mission you successfully perform for the entity, you are rewarded with treasure worth 250 GP (or more, at the DM's discretion). Your patron might reward you with Common or Uncommon magic items, especially consumables such as potions and **Spell Scroll**.

If that check succeeds, you then gain a bonus to all checks made to influence that creature for 1 hour; the bonus is equal to half your level. Once you use this benefit, you can't do so again until 7 days have passed.

#### A Magical Entity Campaign

A campaign driven by a magical entity patron might unfold according to this outline:

1. **Levels 1–4** The campaign begins as the characters stumble into the entity's domain and prove themselves against creatures the entity considers enemies. Aberrations such as **Grell**, **Grick**, and **Grimlock** are all thematically appropriate foes.
2. **Levels 5–10** The magical entity approaches the characters or invites them to the entity's lair and offers to become their group patron, sending them on missions against monstrous foes. Githyanki and slaadi make good antagonists for this tier of play.
3. **Levels 11–16** The mysterious mastermind controlling these Aberrations—whom the characters haven't yet identified—captures or imprisons the magical entity, perhaps through the use of an Artifact, and the characters must rescue them. The characters battle **Aboleth**, **Beholder**, **Death Tyrant**, and **Mind Flayer Arcanist**.
4. **Levels 17–20** The mysterious mastermind is revealed as a creature similar to the group patron, but different in nature. For example, Bahamut is captured by a group of **Ancient Red Dragon**. Or Strahd is at the mercy of a powerful **Lich**, such as Acererak or Vecna. These two liches are further described in *Vecna: Eve of Ruin*, including game statistics for Vecna.

### Political Faction

A political faction group patron is tied to the governing structure of a magical realm. A political faction usually has great wealth and access to the realm's resources, but the faction leaders are politicians and bureaucrats, not adventurers. Even if the leaders are powerful mages, they have no interest in risking their own lives. Instead, they hire the characters as spies or agents.

Campaigns with a political faction group patron combine high magic with political intrigue. The characters might be kept in the dark by their patron faction, which gives them only the information they absolutely need (and sometimes not even that). The faction sends the party into dangerous situations that force them to improvise.

*[Page 69]*

#### Political Faction Motivations

A political faction might patronize a group for the following reasons:

1. **The Characters Are Residents** If the characters live in the realm where the faction governs, the faction believes the characters are easier to recruit and more likely to be loyal. The faction might exert legal pressure on the characters, forcing them to do as the faction requires.
2. **The Characters Are Foreign** The faction prefers to hire outsiders who can operate in neighboring realms without attracting attention, and who know the languages and customs of the faction's rivals.
3. **The Characters Are Deniable Assets** The political faction hires the adventures without official sanction. The party is sent on dangerous missions in far-off lands, and if they're captured, the political faction denies any connection or responsibility for the party's actions.

*[Page 69]*

#### Political Faction Patrons

![The Red Wizards of Thay are a dangerous and powerful group patron. (Art: Magali Villeneuve)](book/AU/040-03-013.red-wizards-of-thay.webp)

The following organizations, each detailed in *Forgotten Realms: Heroes of Faerûn*, could serve as political faction group patrons:

1. **Red Wizards** The Red Wizards rule Thay, a land on the eastern edge of Faerûn in the Forgotten Realms. Thay is expansionist, and its rulers are mostly cruel tyrants. Thay and the Red Wizards are expanded on in *Arcana Unleashed: Deadfall*, which includes an adventure that embroils characters in a mission to kill *Szass Tam*, a Red Wizard lich who plots to once again control the nation.
2. **Simbarch Council** Thay's neighbor, the land of Aglarond, is ruled by a group of sorcerers called the Simbarch Council. In addition to governing the realm, the council trains sorcerers who come to Aglarond from across Faerûn. Aglarond has a long history of military conflict with Thay.
3. **Witches of Rashemen** Rashemen is a land on Thay's northern border. Rashemen is ruled by masked warlocks known as Wychlaran, who make pacts with ancient spirits that dwell in Rashemen's dense and magical forests. Like Aglarond, Rashemen has been invaded repeatedly by Thay, and some of the witches occasionally wish to strike at their longtime enemies.

Give your faction at least one rival group in the same realm or a neighboring one; many adventures the characters undertake should be missions against a rival.

### Trickster

Tricksters are powerful beings—often archfey—who are known for their whimsical nature. A trickster can take many forms and might appear pleasant, angry, or simply otherworldly. But at their core, the trickster is unpredictable, their priorities and goals changing with the seasons or even from day to day.

Tricksters make good patrons for groups that prefer social interaction, exploration, and nonviolent conflict resolution over combat. Many tricksters prefer humiliating and pranking their enemies to killing them. Groups with a trickster patron are likely to spend time in the *Feywild*, visiting Domains of Delight and learning the distinctive rules of each.

*[Page 72]*

#### Trickster Motivations

A trickster might patronize a group for the following reasons:

1. **The Characters Are Interesting** Tricksters are like magpies, driven to collect shiny baubles. Adventurers are colorful, larger-than-life personalities who are likely to attract a trickster's attention.
2. **The Trickster Needs Plausible Deniability** A trickster makes enemies, and those enemies often instantly recognize the trickster and are alert to disguises. The trickster needs agents who aren't known to be associated with the trickster.
3. **The Characters Are Expendable Assets** The trickster needs dangerous tasks accomplished and is unwilling to send their more experienced agents out of concern those agents might not return. The characters' deaths won't inconvenience the trickster.

![Tasha is a trickster patron whose myriad alliances shroud her motivations in mystery. (Art: Caroline Gariba)](book/AU/041-03-014.tasha.webp)

*[Page 73]*

#### Trickster Patrons

The following characters from the D&D multiverse, all briefly described in the *Dungeon Master's Guide*, could serve as trickster patrons:

1. **Queen of Air and Darkness** The Queen of Air and Darkness is a foreboding and mysterious archfey who rules the Gloaming Court, a Domain of Delight in the *Feywild*. She favors followers who are willful and follow their instincts, obeying no laws. She's likely to give the characters total freedom to choose how to accomplish their missions.
2. **Summer Queen** Titania, the Summer Queen, is a benevolent figure, but she nonetheless displays all the whimsical arbitrariness of other archfey. The Summer Court is infamous for its elaborate rules of behavior; characters working for Titania inevitably break unstated rules, prompting Titania to assign them punishments that lead to further adventures.
3. **Tasha** *Tasha*, the adopted daughter of Baba Yaga, is a mighty spellcaster who authored the *Demonomicon* and invented several famous spells. Tasha has a unique relationship with fate, having been a tyrant known as Iggwilv, the Witch Queen of Perrenland; a planes-walking archmage; and Zybilna, archfey of the domain of Prismeer. You can learn more about Tasha in *The Wild Beyond the Witchlight*. Her mysterious goals could lead the characters anywhere in the multiverse.

Your trickster should focus their practical jokes and pranks on rivals and enemies, not the characters, or the party will soon find the trickster tiresome rather than amusing. Ensure that the trickster (mostly) tells the truth to the characters while maintaining a sense of humor and indulging the characters' pranks and jests.

The trickster has formed many surprising relationships in their travels across the multiverse, and they can call upon unexpected allies to aid your missions. ### Wizards' Circle

Powerful spellcasters occasionally form an alliance known as a wizards' circle to pursue goals beyond the reach of any individual. Wizards' circles are often temporary, formed to resolve a specific problem that threatens the world or the multiverse. But even archmages need friends or allies, and the members of a wizards' circle might make their alliance permanent.

In many ways, a wizards' circle is like an archmage group patron, with the added complication that the circle consists of several strong-willed individuals who don't necessarily like each other. The characters get caught in the middle of long debates and old rivalries between members of the circle, and it can be hard to satisfy one member of the circle without alienating another.

*[Page 75]*

#### Wizards' Circle Motivations

A wizards' circle might patronize a group for the following reasons:

1. **The Characters Balance Each Other** Each member of the wizards' circle has their own preferences when selecting agents and operatives. The party represents a collection of these preferences, with each member of the circle backing one member of the party. Although each character is backed by a single member, the circle as a whole serves as a group patron for the characters.
2. **The Characters Have Special Skills** A wizards' circle lacks expertise in fields outside members' specialties, including types of magic outside their bailiwick, martial prowess, or exploration skills. The characters provide useful abilities that the circle members lack.
3. **Pawns on the Chessboard** The wizards' circle supports the group because the characters feature in the circle's long-term plans. The circle members might know one of the characters is unknowingly a noble and thus a potential candidate for the throne, or they might have identified one of the characters as the only person who can safely wield a dangerous Artifact. These facts might not be known to the characters.

![Notoriously argumentative mages Jallarzi Sallavarian, Bigby, and Mordenkainen unite when a dire threat to their world arises. (Art: Ina Wong)](book/AU/042-03-015.jallarzi-bigby-and-mordenkainen.webp)

*[Page 76]*

#### Wizards' Circle Patrons

The following organizations could serve as wizards' circle group patrons:

1. **Circle of Eight** The Circle of Eight is an alliance of powerful spellcasters that includes the gnome *Bigby*; the humans *Jallarzi Sallavarian*, *Mordenkainen*, and *Otiluke*; and the dwarf *Otto*, all briefly described in the *Dungeon Master's Guide*. The Circle is based in the world of Greyhawk and concerns itself with maintaining the power balance between Oerth's realms and factions. The Circle of Eight favors neutral-aligned agents and avoids attention, preferring to work behind the scenes.
2. **Order of the Many-Starred Cloak** The Order of the Many-Starred Cloak is dedicated to defending and supporting Neverwinter, a city in the Forgotten Realms. In the past, the order has protected the city from rival mages in nearby Luskan, among other threats. Members include powerful mages such as the human Gallio Elibro, who's known for his planar research. The order might hire characters to investigate threats against the city, such as monsters amassed near Neverwinter or cults ensconced in the city.
3. **Wizards Three** The wizard Alustriel Silverhand called on her friends Tasha and Mordenkainen to thwart one of Vecna's plots. This alliance is known as the Wizards Three, and it is detailed in *Vecna: Eve of Ruin*. In that adventure, one of the Wizards Three isn't who they appear to be; you might continue this deception or decide the three wizards continue their alliance once Vecna is defeated.

*[Page 76]*

#### Creating Your Own Wizards' Circle

Instead of using one of the wizards' circles described above, you can create a wizards' circle to serve as a group patron for your characters. You can use several of the archmage stat blocks in **chapter 6**, each of which specializes in one school of magic. If your world has a mages' guild, discussed earlier in this chapter, that guild might make an excellent wizards' circle group patron.

To make your own wizards' circle, start by creating one circle member for each of the player characters; this member acts as a personal patron to that character, while the circle as a whole supports the party's activities. For example, if one of your players has created a Sorcerer with the Draconic Sorcery subclass, the circle might include a dragon who has chosen the sorcerer as their personal agent. Other circle members, however, support different characters, and these members are the dragon's rivals.

When you create a wizards' circle for your campaign, you can roll on or choose from the Wizards' Circle Type table.

| 1d6 | Patron Type |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Council of the Wise. The circle members are the world's mightiest, most benevolent mages, but the most charismatic of them is secretly a traitor. Most of the characters' missions are altruistic, but occasionally the traitor sends them on missions meant to sow discord among the group. |
| 2 | Disaster Preventers. The circle members don't like each other, but they've come together to stop the apocalypse. They need the characters to achieve lesser missions while they focus on the larger threat. |
| 3 | Grudging Allies. Longtime enemies, the circle members wreaked great destruction during their conflicts. They've agreed to avoid fighting each other, instead using the characters to pursue the goals they can agree on. |
| 4 | Pub Crew. The circle formed as an excuse to drink and socialize. Members have no time for dangerous missions to save the world, sending the characters instead. |
| 5 | War Veterans. The circle members are veterans who have served together in several wars. In their retirement, they prefer to send more energetic operatives—the characters—to stop the next great war. |
| 6 | Well-Meaning Imposters. The circle members are frauds with only minor magical power. They maintain this ruse because without the circle, the realm would collapse into warring factions. If the characters learn of the wizards' lack of ability, they might continue to work for the circle to avoid larger conflicts. |

#### Wizards' Circle Patron Perks

Characters in a party whose patron is a wizards' circle gain the following perks.

*[Page 76]*

##### Assisting Apprentices

The wizards in the circle have trained numerous apprentices, many of whom are loyal to their mentor. Whenever you're in a city, a former apprentice of a circle member can cast any level 1 or lower spell at no cost, as long as you identify yourself as an agent of that circle member. A former apprentice can cast any level 2–8 spell for you at half the usual cost, as long as you're in a city and similarly identify yourself.

*[Page 76]*

##### Compensation

The wizards' circle pays you to complete tasks on the circle's behalf. On average, the wizards' circle pays each member of your group 2 GP per day (enough to sustain a *Comfortable* lifestyle). Or you might be paid (at least 300 GP) after completing each task the wizards' circle assigns.

*[Page 77]*

##### Magical Resources

The wizards' circle has collected untold magic items and magical curiosities. This collection is scattered among the sanctums of the circle members. A wizards' circle can give members of the group any Common or Uncommon magic item in the *Dungeon Master's Guide*, as long as possession of the item would help the group's tasks. When the characters reach level 11, the wizards' circle can retrieve one Rare and one Very Rare magic item in between the group's tasks and award it to them as additional compensation. When the characters reach level 17, the wizards' circle can retrieve one Rare, Very Rare, or Legendary magic item in between tasks and award it to the group as additional compensation.

*[Page 77]*

##### Safe Houses

The wizards' circle maintains magically warded safe houses and hiding spots throughout the world or across multiple worlds. Your group knows how to locate these hidden redoubts and can sustain a *Modest* lifestyle in one for no cost. Revealing the location of one of these safe houses, whether purposefully or by accident, causes you to lose favor with the wizards' circle, and you might be banned from using them.

*[Page 77]*

#### Wizards' Circle Contacts

The members of a wizards' circle don't always agree, and they keep secrets from each other. An individual in the circle might meet with only one or two characters at a time, in secret. In addition, the circle as a whole has an official spokesperson who communicates with the characters and gives them tasks.

Roll on or choose from the Wizards' Circle Contact table to determine who manages the relationship between the characters and the wizards' circle.

#### A Wizards' Circle Campaign

A campaign driven by a wizards' circle patron might unfold according to this outline:

1. **Levels 1–4** Each character in the group is selected by a single member of the wizards' circle and brought together to serve the circle. Early missions pit the group against opposing **Mage Apprentice**, **Nothic**, and other magical threats.
2. **Levels 5–10** When the characters seek to recover magical lore, obtain dangerous Artifacts, or slay magical foes, sometimes the object of their quest is missing or has already fled. It slowly becomes clear that someone inside the circle is feeding information to the circle's enemies.
3. **Levels 11–16** The traitorous member of the circle reveals themself and seizes control of the circle and its resources. Other members of the circle are imprisoned, slain, bought off, or cowed into working with the traitor. The characters might play along for a while or go on the run, but eventually they must confront the traitor and their allies.
4. **Levels 17–20** Now that the traitorous member of the circle is dealt with, the circle has openings for new members. The characters join the circle, recruiting new agents and rounding up the traitor's remaining followers.
